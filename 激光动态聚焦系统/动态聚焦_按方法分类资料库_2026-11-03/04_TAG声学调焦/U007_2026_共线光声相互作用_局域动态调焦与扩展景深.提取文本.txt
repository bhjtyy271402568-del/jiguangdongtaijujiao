Localized dynamic lensing with instantaneous extended depth of field 
via collinear light–sound interactions

Abstract
Tunable acousto-optic (AO) lenses have recently emerged as versatile tools for optical beam shaping, imaging, and parti-
cle manipulation. Conventional AO lenses rely on light propagating orthogonally to a standing ultrasonic field, producing 
Bessel-like beam patterns via the Raman–Nath effect that compromise focal localization and depth of field (DoF). Here, we 
introduce a novel class of AO lenses based on a three-dimensional, dynamically variable refractive index profile generated 
by a z-axis-scanning focused ultrasound transducer. By exploiting co- and counter-propagating light–sound interactions 
over an extended axial range, we achieve fully controllable, localized optical focusing with an instantaneous extended DoF. 
We demonstrate dynamic tuning of focal position, lateral resolution, and optical power throughput by adjusting ultrasound 
parameters. This approach offers a promising platform for applications requiring precise remote focusing, three-dimensional 
micromanipulation, and deep tissue imaging or therapy.

Introduction
Lenses with variable focal lengths are essential in various 
fields of science and technology, ranging from imaging to 
sensing and laser beam shaping to microscopy, adaptive 
optics, and materials processing [1]. Over the years, various 
techniques have been deployed to realize dynamic focusing, 
including mechanically moving and micro-electro-mechan-
ical systems devices, electro-optically addressable holo-
graphic plates, and acousto-optic (AO) elements. Among 
them, AO approaches are highly preferred [2, 3] for provid-
ing fast switching, adaptability (e.g., in shape and dioptric 
power), and noninvasiveness with no need for macroscopic 
moving elements [4].
The AO effect refers to light diffraction due to the varia-
tions in refractive index caused by the presence of ultrasound 
waves [5–7]. Several diffractive AO elements and acoustic 
gradient-index lenses have been introduced in recent years, 
generating tunable Bessel-like spots by forming ultrasonic 
cylindrical standing waves that interact orthogonally with the 
passing light [3, 4, 8–14] —an effect known as Raman-Nath 
[15]. By using a confined liquid-filled radial-mode-vibrating 
piezoelectric shell, these AO lenses enable a combination of 
tunability, large damage thresholds, and fast z-focus scan-
ning [10, 14]. Such fast z-focusing is accompanied by virtual 
extended Depth of Field (DoF) owing to collecting multiple 
focal planes [14]. However, the effective numerical aperture 
(NA) of these lenses is low—as the aperture is limited by the 
central lobe of the Bessel function that describes the ultra-
sound standing wave in the piezo-shell [14]. Also, in addi-
tion to generating transversely non-localized (in x–y plane) 
ring-shaped focal spots, these arrangements tend to suffer 
from the inability to change the refractive index profile in the 
z-direction and outside of the AO lens chamber while relying 
on pulsed laser sources for synchronization. Finally, unlike 
the ideal Bessel beams that are diffraction-free, the beam, in 
this case, tends to diffract quickly [16].
Here we present a novel AO dynamic lensing approach 
that exploits collinear co- or counter-propagating light and 
focused ultrasound waves to produce a unique, spherically 
confined refractive index profile for tunable optical focusing. 
Unlike conventional AO systems that rely on standing-wave 
Bessel-like fields, our design generates a transversely local-
ized acoustic focal zone to achieve a true single-point optical 
focus. To realize this, we developed a custom high-frequency 
z-axis-scan focused ultrasound (FUS) transducer, designed 
to form a single-point acoustic focus outside and in front of 
the transducer aperture (Fig. 1b). The thin piezoelectric disk 
on the front face of the FUS transducer operates in thickness-
mode axial vibration (Fig. 2d), producing traveling ultrasonic 
waves that enhance longitudinal light–sound interaction and 
induce a 3D-variable refractive index profile (Fig. 2a-b). 
This configuration enables both laterally localized dynamic 
focusing and instantaneous extended DoF for continuous-
wave (CW) laser beams, without requiring laser–ultrasound 
synchronization. As illustrated in Fig. 1a, the laser beam is 
expanded and directed through the water medium to interact 
with the focused acoustic pressure field generated by the FUS 
transducer at modest acoustic intensities. Figure 2 schemati-
cally depicts the light–sound interaction and induced refrac-
tive index gradients that underpin the lensing mechanism.

Experimental setup
The FUS transducer has an aperture size of 24 mm, with 
an acoustic focal length of 23 mm (in front of it). It was 
designed and fabricated with PZT-8 (a high mechanical-Q 
material) and a final thickness of 0.46 mm. The electrical 
circuit, as well as matching and backing layers (made from 
epoxy) were placed inside a custom-machined brass hous-
ing. The average center frequency of the transducer was 
3.3 MHz, and the − 6-dB bandwidth was 51%, as estimated 
from the measured pulse-echo response. The average inser-
tion loss was measured to be 16.3 dB.
In the characterization setup, the Gaussian beam from 
a He–Ne class-II laser (Oriel Corporation) with an output 
power of 0.95 mW, centered at a wavelength of 633.2 nm, 
was expanded, and collimated with a beam expander to a 
beam diameter of 6.12 mm. The laser was direct to a raised 
fish tank filled halfway with deionized milli-pure water. 
The FUS transducer was submerged in the water upward 
with the focal face pointing away from the bottom of the 
tank (for counter-propagating setup, Figs. 1 and supple-
mentary material 3). FUS transducer was arranged either in 
a co-propagating configuration (Fig. S1) or a counter-prop-
agating configuration (Fig. 1 and supplementary material 3) 
relative to the laser-beam propagation, with the beam enter-
ing through the bottom of the fish tank. A photograph of 
the experimental setup is shown in supplementary material 
3, showing the tank, FUS transducer, laser source, CCD 
camera, and related equipment. Table 2 summarizes the 
key characteristics of the experimental setup.

In addition to the reduction in laser beam width and 
its increased focusing, the extension of the DoF is one of 
the key features of the ultrasonic interaction with the laser 
beam. The dynamic control of focal properties in our system 
arises from the interaction between the FUS pressure field 
and the optical beam. The acoustic pressure amplitude ­(Pac) 
is approximately proportional to both the input voltage (V) 
and the duty cycle (D), since higher duty cycles deliver more 
average power to the medium. This acoustic field induces a 
spatially varying refractive index contrast in the medium, 
governed by the relation Δn = C·Pac, where C is the acou-
sto-optic coefficient. A larger Δn corresponds to a stronger 
refractive index gradient, which bends light more sharply, 
leading to a shorter optical focal length and a narrower DoF. 
This relationship can be summarized approximately by the 
following proportionalities:
foptical ∝1∕Δn(V, D) ∝1∕(V ⋅D), and DoF ∝foptical

dating these principles. Figure 9 presents measurements of 
optical beam diameter and FWHM in the x and y directions 
along the propagation axis for two different FUS duty cycles 
(2% and 20%), both at a constant voltage of 84 V. Panel 
(a) corresponds to the 2% duty cycle and (b) to the 20% 
duty cycle. Panels (a,b) compare the depth of focus (DoF),

During-FUS results (green) show significantly narrowed 
beam diameters and FWHM compared to the unmodulated 
cases (brown). These results confirm that lower duty cycles 
produce longer instantaneous DoF with sharper focus.
By stating that the extended DoF is instantaneous, we 
mean that the extended optical focus appears physically 
and in real time—unlike in TAG lens systems, where it is 
achieved by rapidly scanning multiple focal planes and inte-
grating them into a single image, thereby creating a virtually 
extended DoF. In contrast, our C2AM system generates a 
true, static extended DoF without scanning, due to the col-
linear alignment and interaction of the focused ultrasound 
and laser beams. We refer to this as instantaneous DoF. Fur-
ther investigation is ongoing to fully understand the mecha-
nism behind this behavior.
Figure 10 shows the effect of varying the FUS input 
voltage on the axial location of the optical focal spot. At 
the maximum input voltage of 105 V (panel a, duty cycle 
5%), the focal point is observed at z = 4 cm. As the volt-
age decreases to 84 V (panel b, duty cycle 2%) and 63 V 
(panel c, duty cycle 20%), the focal spot shifts progres-
sively farther away to z = 8 cm and z = 10 cm, respectively. 
Despite the differences in duty cycle across these condi-
tions, the results indicate that the input voltage has a more 
pronounced effect on the axial focal position than the duty 
cycle. The relationship among focal length, voltage, and 
duty cycle appears nonlinear, likely due to complex inter-
actions between acoustic pressure dynamics and medium 
response under varying power levels.

Our experimental results demonstrate dynamic and tunable 
control of key optical parameters—focal point location, DoF, 
lateral resolution, FWHM, and power throughput—by adjust-

Together, these results demonstrate that our system enables 
flexible and precise tuning of optical focusing performance, 
balancing resolution, DoF, focal location, and power delivery

To compare our C2AM system with conventional AO 
lenses, particularly Tunable Acoustic Gradient (TAG) 
lenses, we evaluated both the effective NA and refractive 
index modulation depth (Δn). The NA in our system is esti-
mated from the measured FWHM of the optical focus using 
the relation NA ≈ 0.51 × λ/FWHM, where λ = 633.2 nm. 
With a minimum measured FWHM of 0.16  mm, we 
obtain NA ≈ 0.0078, which is significantly higher than 
the typical range reported for TAG lenses in the literature 
(NA ~ 0.0005–0.001) [9]. The improved confinement in our 
system arises from the traveling-wave focused ultrasound 
field, which is not limited by the central lobe size inherent to 
the standing-wave Bessel beam in TAG lenses [14].
Furthermore, the refractive index modulation depth in 
our system is estimated as Δn = C. ­Ppeak [2], where C ≈ 
1.3 × 10⁻1⁰ Pa⁻1 is the AO coefficient of water. Using the 
relation I = ­(Prms​)2/(ρc), the root-mean-square pressure is 
calculated as ­Prms​≈19.36 MPa, giving P_peak ≈ 27.4 MPa 
at 105 V, which yields Δn ≈ 3.56 × 10⁻3. This is at least an 
order of magnitude greater than TAG lenses, which typically 
achieve Δn on the order of 10⁻5–10⁻4 [9, 13].

It is worth noting that the acoustic wave frequency, the 
size of the piezoelectric element, and the sound velocity in 
the medium influence the lens switching speed [9]. The thin 
piezoelectric material in our z-axis-scan FUS transducer, 
which supports higher resonance frequencies, is expected to 
enable faster focal switching than existing AO elements [10]. 
Potentially, up to two orders of magnitude higher switch-
ing speed could be achieved using even thinner disks. Fur-
thermore, the electromechanical coupling coefficient of 
our transducer is nominally higher than that of cylindrical 
transducers commonly used in AO lenses, favoring lower 
insertion loss, wider frequency response, improved axial 
resolution, and higher acoustic penetration [19–21].
Our results demonstrate that the maximum applied inten-
sity (25 kW/cm2) at 105 V remains below the cavitation 
threshold in air-free water [22], avoiding cavitation and 
Mie scattering effects. This ensures that the observed opti-
cal focusing performance in our experiments is not compro-
mised by bubble formation.
To enable future translation of our approach to deep tissue 
imaging, it is important to consider the potential for cavita-
tion, particularly when using pulsed laser irradiation, which 
achieves higher peak powers but also increases the risk of 
cavitation due to rapid thermal expansion and acoustic tran-
sients. Our preliminary simulations and tissue experiments 
indicate that CW laser operation enables improved optical 
focusing at ultrasound frequencies above 2–3 MHz, while 
keeping cavitation risk minimal. Nevertheless, the cavitation 
threshold is expected to vary depending on tissue proper-
ties and operating parameters, and further investigation is 
warranted. In addition, our system offers flexibility to miti-
gate cavitation risks and improve portability. Redesigning 
the FUS transducers—for example, using smaller apertures, 
lower voltage drives, or alternative frequency ranges—can 
reduce acoustic intensities while maintaining sufficient 
refractive index modulation.
We also plan to explore alternative coupling media with 
higher elasto-optic coefficients and higher refractive indices, 
including both liquids and solids, which could enhance lens-
ing efficiency and allow for a smaller, more compact con-
tainer. Since our FUS transducers are already compact and 
fabricated in-house, further miniaturization and optimization

In earlier studies [16], the light and ultrasound paths were 
either perpendicular, as in the Raman-Nath scattering regime 
([8], foundational to TAG lens technology), or at a small 
Bragg angle, still near-perpendicular. In our configuration, 
the Bragg angle for first-order diffraction can be calculated as 
follows. Assuming a light wavelength in vacuum λ₀ = 633 nm, 
refractive index of water nₘ ≈ 1.33, ultrasound frequency 
fₛ = 3.3 MHz, and speed of sound cₛ ≈ 1500 m/s, the light 
wavelength in water is λ = λ₀/nₘ ≈ 476 nm, and the acous-
tic wavelength is Λ = cₛ/fₛ ≈ 454.5 µm. Using Bragg’s law: 
nλ = 2Λ·sinθ. For first-order diffraction (n = 1): sinθ ≈ λ/(2Λ) 
≈ 5.237 × 10⁻4, yields θ ≈ 0.03°. This calculation confirms 
that the angle between ultrasound and light in prior configu-
rations is close to 90°, while our system operates in a truly 
co-linear configuration with ultrasound and laser beams either 
co-propagating or counter-propagating.
This co- and counter-propagating configuration enables 
distinct focal behaviors and enhanced longitudinal inter-
action. In our experiments, the counter-propagating setup 
consistently produced a more stable, sharply defined focus 
with higher resolution, likely due to asymmetries in tank 
alignment or optical path, which we plan to address in future 
compact setups. Additionally, mid-range interaction angles 
may offer a balance between stability and confinement, 
which we intend to explore using phased arrays or adjust-
able transducer orientations.
Conclusion
We have demonstrated a novel class of dynamic AO lenses 
based on co-linear interaction between a laser beam and a 
focused traveling ultrasound field (C2AM system). This 
approach enables a physically localized optical focus in the 
lateral plane and an extended DoF along the propagation 
axis, realized instantaneously without scanning or compu-
tational reconstruction. By tuning the ultrasound input volt-
age and duty cycle, we achieved flexible, precise control of 
key optical parameters—including focal point location, DoF, 
lateral resolution, FWHM, and optical power throughput. 
Lower duty cycles favored sharper focus and longer DoF, 
whereas higher duty cycles enhanced optical power at the 
expense of resolution. The axial focal position was primarily 
governed by input voltage, enabling additional tunability.
The co- and counter-propagating geometries distinguish 
this system from conventional AO lenses, offering higher 
index modulation, tighter confinement, and broad tunability. 
These features make C2AM a promising platform for imag-
ing, manipulation, and photonic applications, while laying 
the groundwork for future compact and tissue-compatible 
implementations.
