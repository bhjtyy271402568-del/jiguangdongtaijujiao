Abstract: High-speed laser processing of curved surfaces is limited by laser three-dimensional
(3D) scanning equipment. It is difficult to balance efficiency and uniformity during processing.
In this paper, a self-developed high-speed triple galvanometer laser 3D scanning system (TGLSS)
was used. The equations for the laser energy distribution and the spatial spot overlap rate at
different positions of the curved surface were derived. The laser interaction mechanism with
curved materials and the ablation effect were investigated. Scanning experiments on cylindrical
surfaces were conducted to verify the processing differences of the laser at different positions of
the curved surface, and the processing quality of the surface was improved by using a variable
speed filling method.

1.
Introduction
As an environmentally friendly technology, lasers are widely used in material processing such
as microstructure engraving [1–3], coating removal [4–6], drilling [7–9], and texturing [10,11].
To process planar materials, an XY two-dimensional (2D) galvanometer is often used to scan
the laser focus in two mutually orthogonal directions (X- and Y-directions) perpendicular to
the optical axis. This method allows scanning speeds of up to 10 m/s at a focal length of
160 mm [12]. As production and application requirements escalate to three-dimensional (3D)
space, the angle of incidence at which the laser is focused on the curved surface may affect the
manufacturing efficiency of the product, the precise shaping of the structure, and the effectiveness
of the functional surface [13]. Higher scanning speeds of the laser focus along the optical axis
(Z-direction) are required for the study of this effect. The conventional method achieves efficient
processing of curved parts by combining a 2D galvanometer with a multi-axis CNC, utilizing
the high speed of the galvanometer and the flexibility of a multi-axis system [14,15]. However,
the Z-direction focusing capability of the multi-axis CNC is difficult to match the dynamic
performance of the galvanometer. This limits the 3D scanning speed of the system and its ability
to process curved parts, and limits the study of the scanning process of the laser along the contour
of the surface.
The focus scanning speed in the Z-direction can be increased by moving the negative lens
along the optical axis, but the Z-focusing speed in this method is largely limited by the speed at
which the optics and their accessories can be moved, which is up to three orders of magnitude

to the electrical wetting [19,20] method or the shape-variable lens [21,22] principle allows the
Z-direction position of the laser focus to be moved faster. However, the surface shape of a variable
lens is difficult to control precisely. Alvarez lenses [23–25] shift focus by moving the position of
the lens, but it is difficult to meet the need for continuous and linear focusing. A high-speed triple
galvanometer laser 3D scanning system (TGLSS) achieves the same scanning capability in the
X-, Y-, and Z-directions using three highly dynamic characteristic galvanometers, as described in
our previous work [26,27]. The high-speed TGLSS improves the processing efficiency of curved
parts at a similar cost and provides the ability to scan the laser focus directly along the surface
contour, which is difficult to achieve at high speeds with conventional systems.
In this paper, a self-developed TGLSS was used, which could increase the laser scanning
speed in the Z-direction to the same speed as that in the X- and Y-directions, providing sufficient
conditions for realizing the efficient processing of curved parts. Compared with our previous
work, we further derive the spot energy distribution of the laser focus on curved surfaces with
different incidence angles. Equations for the spatial spot overlap rate at different incidence
angles and speeds when scanning along a curved surface contour were derived. The effects of
incidence angle and scanning speed on ablation under 3D scanning were verified by simulation
and experiment. Based on the spot overlap rate at different incidence angles, a variable-speed
laser 3D scanning filling method was developed based on the TGLSS to balance the scanning
efficiency and uniformity of curved parts. Parts with cylindrical surfaces were processed using
the system, and the efficient 3D scanning capability of the system was verified.
2.
Experimental system and materials
2.1.
High-speed triple galvanometer laser 3D scanning system
The TGLSS used to perform laser scanning experiments was shown in Fig. 1(a). It consisted
of two off-axis parabolic mirrors PM1 and PM2 (35588, Edmund), three galvanometers G1,
G2, and G3 (ExtraScan II 14, Hans), and an F-theta lens. The PMs had a focal length f of
152.4 mm and an off-axis angle of 45°. The G1, G2, and G3 could be rotated by ±12.5°. The
F-theta lens with a focal length f FL of 100 mm was used to focus the laser. A nanosecond fiber
laser (YDFLP-100-M8-M-R, JPT) with a beam diameter of 3.0 mm (expanded to 4.5 mm), a
wavelength λ of 1064 nm, a beam quality factor M2 of 1.4, and a maximum average power of
100 W was used for the experiments. The system could scan at speeds of up to 10 m/s in the X-,
Y- and Z-directions. As shown in Figs. 1(b) and (c), a cylindrical sample was used to test the
focusing effect of the laser scanning along the surface contour. The radius R of the cylindrical
surface was 25 mm, and the scanning path was varied along the surface (based on the TGLSS’s
high-speed Z-focusing capability) with a centroid angle ∆α of 45°. A round trip fill mode with
line spacing s of 200 µm was used.
2.2.
Materials and experimental parameters
In the experiments, commercial aluminum films were tested in the experiment by attaching them
to the sample, as shown in Fig. (d). In order to investigate the effect of position of the curved
surface on the laser processing, orthogonal experiments were carried out using four different
incidence angles α: 0°/15°30°/45° and speed from 1 m/s to 5 m/s. The laser scanning path
was a rectangle on a cylindrical plane with a line spacing of 200 µm. The experimental laser
power was 50 W, and the laser pulse frequency was 150 kHz, and the experimental laser scanning
parameters were shown in Table 1. The scanning effects and depth profiles were observed and
compared using a laser scanning confocal microscope (VK-X1100, Keyence). Micrographs of
sample ablation were observed using a scanning electron microscope (Nova NanoSEM 450, FEI).

Results and discussion
4.1.
Surface morphology at different incidence angles and scanning speed
The effect of laser beam processing on curved surface morphology was investigated at different
angles of incidence and scanning speeds. The experimental parameters were shown in Table 1.
The results of surface morphology microscopy under laser at different incidence angles and
scanning speeds were shown in Fig. 6. When the angle of incidence α was constant, the depths of
the scan line decreased with the increase in v from 1 m/s to 5 m/s. This was due to the decrease in
the accumulation time of the laser energy and the number of pulses. As v increased from 1 m/s to
5 m/s, the spot spacing became dispersed from continuous due to a decrease in spot overlap UPS.
When the scanning speed v was constant, the depths of the scan line were almost constant
as α increased from 0° to 30°. This was due to the fact that the change in the α did not change
the accumulation of the laser energy on the curved surface. At α = 45°, the line depth became
shallower, which validated the simulation analysis in Fig. 5. The data and variations in specific
scan line depths were shown in Fig. 7 and Table 3. In the region of lower scanning speed (v = 1
m/s), the depth of the scan line showed longer error bars. This was due to the higher energy
accumulation in this region, which produced more surface oxidation and ablation debris.

4.2.
Microscopic ablation effects at different incidence angles and scanning directions
The magnified SEM micrographs of the ablation effect on curved surfaces by laser beam
processing at different incidence angles and scanning speeds were shown in Fig. 8. As shown in
Figs. 8(a-1) ∼(a-5), (b-1) ∼(b-5), (c-1) ∼(c-5), and (d-1) ∼(d-5), surface oxides and ablation
debris were observed when v = 1 m/s, and the scan line became shallower as the laser scanning
speed v increased. At higher magnification (5000 ×) to observe the ablation morphology at the
intersection of the focusing spots, it could be observed that the high-pressure steam continued to
force the liquid metal at the bottom of the pit to drain around the molten keyhole [30], and the
surface oxides and ablation debris became sparse and there were fewer flowing metals and plumes
as the scanning speed increased. This was due to the shorter time for energy accumulation.
The reasons for the difference in ablation morphologies with changes in α and v were that
higher α increased the spatial spot overlap rate but decreased the energy peak at focusing, resulting
in a shorter time for the aluminum surface to be above the ablation threshold. An increase in v
decreased the spatial spot overlap and the time for energy to accumulate on the curved surface.

4.3.
Laser variable speed processing curved surfaces to improve uniformity
As a result of the above analysis, the effect of laser processing on curved surfaces was influenced
by the combined effect of the processing position on the curved surface and the scanning speed.
By decreasing the scanning speed at the higher incidence speed. By decreasing the scanning
speed at higher incidence angle positions, the spot accumulate time in this region could be
increased to compensate for the decrease in the peak laser value, thus improving the uniformity
of the laser scanning on curved surfaces.
The results and depth profiles of laser scanning on cylindrical samples were shown in Fig. 9.
The scanning line spacing was 10 µm and the laser wavelength λ, power P and pulse frequency f
were shown in Table 1. As shown in Fig. 9(a-2), when the uniform scanning method was used,
the scanning speed v was kept constant at 3 m/s, and the scan line depth decreased with the
increase of the laser incidence angle α. As shown in Fig. 9(b-2), when variable speed scanning
method was used, the scanning speed v decreased from 3 m/s to 2 m/s with the incident angle α
from 0° (top of the cylindrical sample) to 45° (edges of the cylindrical sample), and the scan line
depth was almost unchanged with the increase of the α, which indicated that the uniformity of
the processing was improved. Compared to the conventional method of rotating the scanning
head through a rotary table to compensate for spot shape changes and peak energy reduction, this
method was easier, faster. In addition, this method did not have the problem of focus drift caused
by the inertia of the rotary table.

5.
Conclusion
In this paper, the effect of different positions and different speeds of laser 3D processing of curved
surface on infrared nanosecond laser ablation of aluminum was systematically investigated. The
energy distribution of the laser incident on a curved surface and the spatial spot overlap rate
in scanning along the curved surface contour were derived and modeled. The thermal effects
of laser ablation of curved aluminum at different incidence angles and speeds were simulated,
experimented and analyzed. The scanning uniformity of the laser on curved materials was
improved by developing a filling method with variable speed scanning to compensate for the
low peak energy of the laser in regions with high angles of incidence. A reference was provided
for the further development of laser technology for efficient and uniform processing of curved
surfaces. The technology and equipment were expected to be integrated and applied in aerospace,

automotive and precision manufacturing [31–33]. The industrialized scanning head can be
mounted on the multi-axis mechanism, replacing the conventional laser scanning head (XY
galvanometer and Z-dynamic focusing), and improving the efficiency and accuracy of laser 3D
scanning processing. The main conclusions were as follows:
(1) Based on the method of approximating smaller curved surface areas as inclined planar areas,
the conversion of laser incidence to different positions on the surface to laser incidence on
the plane at different angles of incidence. Combined with the energy distribution equations
of Gaussian beam, the energy distribution model of the laser incident at different positions
of the curved surface was established. As the angle of incidence increased, the peak
energy at the center decreased and the concentration of the energy distribution along the
direction of the curved surface contour decreased, while the concentration of the energy
distribution along the other orthogonal direction remained constant. It should be noted that
for high curvature regions, we can further reduce the length of the approximation region to
minimize the error and thus improve the overall modeling accuracy.
(2) The equations for the spatial spot overlap rate as a function of incident angle and scanning
speed for laser scanning along a curved surface contour were derived. Since the projection
of the spot on the surface was elongated with increasing angle of incidence, the spatial spot
overlap increased with increasing angle of incidence. This trend changed more significantly
at higher scanning speed (> spot radius w0 × pulse frequency f).
(3) The ablation effect of laser scanning at different speeds at different positions on curved
aluminum surfaces were simulated by COMSOL software. It was found that the depth of
the scan line decreased significantly when the laser scanned the higher incidence angle
(α=45°) region of the curved surface, which was due to the decrease of the peak laser
energy resulting in a shorter time for the material to be above the ablation threshold. In
accordance with the conclusion of (2), the spatial spot overlap rate was higher in the higher
incidence angle region, but at α = 45°, the increase in the spatial spot overlap rate could
not compensate for the decrease in the focal peak energy. The above simulations were
confirmed by experiments and microscopic observations.
(4) Summarizing the above analysis, we found that the processing effect of laser on curved
surfaces was affected by the combination of incidence angle and scanning speed. Therefore,
processing curved samples with conventional uniform scanning speed method inevitably
posed problems of processing uniformity. A filling method with variable speed scanning
was designed to reduce the scanning speed at the higher incidence angle position of
the curved surface to compensate for the decrease of peak energy at the focus. The
improvement of uniformity by variable speed scanning was confirmed by performing
uniform (speed = 3 m/s) scanning and variable speed scanning (speed changed from 3 m/s
to 2 m/s) on cylindrical samples, respectively.
