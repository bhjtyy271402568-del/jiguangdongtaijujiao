A B S T R A C T
In this paper, a triple galvanometer laser three-dimensions (3D) scanning system (TGLSS) was proposed. The 
system consisted of three galvanometers and two parabolic mirrors, with rapid movement of the laser focus in 3D 
space by rotations of the three galvanometers. The optical principle of the TGLSS was analyzed, and the method 
of designing TGLSS by geometrical optics and Abbe’s sine condition was given in detail. Scanning range and 
focusing quality were analyzed by optical simulations. An experiment was carried out to verify the feasibility of 
TGLSS. The implications of TGLSS for reflective dynamic focusing device (RDFD)-based laser system and their 
impact on technologically relevant applications were discussed. The great potential for enhancing the speed of 
advanced laser processing in 3D space was offered by TGLSS.
1. Introduction
Laser scanning has a wide range of applications, such as 
manufacturing [1–4], texture processing [5–7], cutting [8–10], cleaning 
[11–14], and imaging [15,16]. The speed and precision of laser scanning 
affect the efficiency and quality of the laser processes [17–19], which 
are the most critical concerns during the processing. For curved parts, 
line width and scanning continuity could affect the dimensional accu­
racy or functional characteristics of the finished product [20,21]. Due to 
its simple structure, high speed, and high precision, a two-axis galva­
nometer is commonly used for two-dimensional (2D) laser scanning 
[22,23]. The scanning speed on the plane perpendicular to the optical 
axis (XY plane) can reach 10 m/s [24]. With the complexity of scanned 
workpieces and requirements, the need for laser scanning has been put 
forward in three dimensions (3D). Achieving a rapid movement of the 
focus in the axial (Z-) direction is the main challenge in laser scanning 
from 2D to 3D. Conventional Z-focusing control methods are mainly 
based on the mechanical movement of optical components [25–27]. The 
Z-focusing speed is significantly limited by the moving speed of the 
optical components and their attachments. Consequently, the scanning 
speed of the laser focus in the Z-direction is three orders of magnitude 
slower than in the X- and Y-directions [28] and the 3D scanning speed of 
laser systems is limited.
Recently, advanced Z-focusing optical elements have emerged to 
meet the needs of industry and science. Methods based on electro- 
wetting [29,30], shape-variable lenses [31–33] or Alvarez lenses 
[34–36] allow for rapid movement of the Z-focus. However, the curva­
tures of the lenses are difficult to control precisely, and the 3D scanning 
speed of the laser systems based on the above varifocal optics is also 
limited to the order of 1 m/s due to the slow response rate [27]. 
Reflective dynamic focusing devices (RDFDs) use two highly dynamic 
properties galvanometers to convert angular motion into axial motion, 
increasing the laser scanning speed in the Z-direction [37–39]. However, 
the optical principle of RDFDs is not well discussed, the relationship 
between structure and aberration has not been analyzed, and no sys­
tematic design method has been proposed. In addition, an RDFD-based 
laser 3D scanning system requires a total of four galvanometers, 
resulting in high costs, large volume and requiring four control channels.
In this paper, the optical principle for RDFDs was analyzed and the 
design method was proposed. A triple galvanometer laser 3D scanning 
system (TGLSS) was proposed based on the method, with 3D scanning 
speed up to 10 m/s. Specifically, as shown in Fig. 1, two parabolic

2. Optical design
2.1. Design and analysis of Z-focus module
The design scheme diagram of the Z-focus module of a TGLSS and 
part of the geometrical optical path model were shown in Fig. 2. The D 
was the laser beam diameter. The PM1 and PM2 were two parabolic 
mirrors with the same focal length f and off-axis angle α. G1 and G2 were 
the galvanometers placed at the focal points of PM1 and PM2, whose 
rotary axes passed through the focal points of PMs. The rotation angles 
of G1 and G2 were equivalent, and the rotation directions were opposite, 
denoted by Δα and −Δα respectively. So that the deflection angles of the 
optical paths were 2Δα and −2Δα respectively. Since the edge rays from 
G1 were not pass through the focal point of PM1, the edge rays exiting 
PM1 were not parallel but converge between PM1 and PM2 to form the 
primary focus F.
When the galvanometers were rotated, the path of F formed a tilted 
image plane between PM1 and PM2, as shown by the blue solid line in 
Fig. 2(a). When F located in the middle position of PM1 and PM2, the Z- 
focus module was a symmetric structure, which satisfied the Abbe sine 
condition [40], in which case the focus had no coma and spherical ab­
errations. For the α corresponding to this F, the distance L between PM1 
and PM2 in this case was LBest. When G1 and G2 were rotated around α 
corresponding to LBest, the object plane of PM2 and the image plane of 
PM1 do not coincide, as shown by the blue dashed line in Fig. 2(a). When 
the primary focus F’ was in the positive focusing region, the focus was 
shifted in the + Z direction, and when the primary focus F’’ was in the 
negative focusing region, the focus was tuned in the −Z direction and 
focused by the FL, as shown in the focal planes (FPs) FP1, FP2 and FP3 in 
Fig. 2(a).

When the diffraction of the ray was considered, the Strehl ratio 
(STRH) reflected the effect of the laser light on the focusing effect in 
terms of energy [41]. Therefore, the STRH was used to evaluate the 
focusing effect within the scanning range, taking the value of 0 to 1, with 
the closer to 1 indicating better focusing. A STRH ≥0.8 was generally 
considered to correspond to diffraction-limited performance and effec­
tive processing. For STRH≤0.3, the behavior of the STRH became un­
correlated to the processing quality due to large aberrations [41]. The 
STRH at the focus of the ZEMAX simulation was shown in Fig. 4 when f 
and L were varied, and Fig. 4(a)~(c) were for α = 30◦, 45◦, and 60◦, 
respectively. The simulation results showed that for a given value of α, 
there was a region of best focusing effect (the yellow part of the figure), 
and this region varies with f and L.
The red curves in Fig. 4(a)~(c) showed the LBest calculated by Eq. (8). 
With α = 30◦, the LBest increased from 228.708 mm to 457.432 mm when 
f increased from 100 mm to 200 mm. With α = 45◦, the LBest increased

by Eq. (8) were all within the optimal STRH region for the ZEMAX

3. Verification of rapid 3D scanning
3.1. Experiment setup
An experiment was carried out to verify the 3D scanning capability of 
the TGLSS, and the experimental setup is shown in Fig. 8. A nanosecond 
fiber laser (YDFLP-100-M8-M−R, JPT) with a 3.0 mm (expanded to 8 
mm, for observing the experimental results more dramatically) beam 
diameter was adopted for the experiment. The wavelength was 1064 
nm, the beam quality factor M2 was 1.4, and the maximum average 
power was 100 W. The focal length f of the off-axis parabolic mirrors 
(35588, Edmund) was 152.4 mm, and the off-axis angle α was 45◦, 
corresponding to the initial state of G1 and G2. The G1, G2 and G3 were 
galvanometers (ExtraScan II 14, Hans), which could be rotated by ±
12.5◦. An F-theta lens with a focal length fFL of 100 mm was used to focus 
the laser. The distance of PM1 and PM2 was 410.729 mm, calculated by 
Eq. (8). An XYZ stage was placed to move the samples. A 45◦block was 
placed on the XYZ stage for tilting an aluminum plate 1060 with a size of 
50 × 50 × 1 mm3, used for the experiment. Three different 3D scanning 
modes were selected to verify the 3D scanning capability of the TGLSS: 
horizontal, tilted, and vertical. The laser scanning path was a 20 × 20 
mm2 rectangle, and each scan was repeated 10 times using the above 
three scanning modes, all with lines spacing of 250 μ m. The laser pro­
cessing effect was used as the criterion for verifying the 3D scanning 
capability. The experimental laser energy was 100 μJ, the laser fre­
quency was 100 kHz, and the 3D scanning speed was 10 m/s. A laser 
scanning confocal microscope (VK-X1100, Keyence) was used to observe 
and compare the scanning effect.

scanning continuity in 3D space, which could help achieve better effi­
ciency and quality of processing [42–48].
4. Conclusion
In conclusion, the optical principle and design method of TGLSS were 
proposed and discussed, and the optical structure of TGLSS with no 
aberration was given by geometrical optics calculation and Abbe’s sine 
condition. The design method could be applied to other similar reflec­
tive dynamic focusing devices (RDFDs). The simulations were per­
formed to analyze the 3D scanning range and effect of TGLSS under 
different parameters, where the axial focusing range ΔZ was varied 
between 7.173 mm and 26.134 mm. In this experimentally verification, 
the improvement in axial scanning speed (10 m/s) enabled different 
laser 3D scanning modes with adjustable line width and scanning con­
tinuity. Other laser processing applications such as manufacturing, 
texture processing, cutting and cleaning will also benefit from this 
development.
