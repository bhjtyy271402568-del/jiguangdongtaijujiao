Extending the 3D scanning range of reflective
dynamic focusing device-based laser scanners
Yi Liu,† Yaqing Qiao,† Yuhang Ding, Wangrong Lu, Tianting Chen, Haoran Ma, Jun
Duan, Wei Xiong,
AND Leimin Deng∗
Wuhan National Laboratory for Optoelectronics, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, Wuhan 430074, China
†These authors contributed equally to this Letter.
*dlm@hust.edu.cn
Received 18 October 2023; revised 21 November 2023; accepted 21 November 2023; posted 21 November 2023; published 8 December 2023
Reflective dynamic focusing devices (RDFDs) have shown
their potential in laser scanning as high-performance laser
Z-direction focusing devices. However, the scanning range
of RDFD-based scanners is limited by aberrations dur-
ing dynamic focusing. An aspheric symmetry correction
(ASC) method was proposed to extend the effective scan-
ning range. An aspheric lens was introduced to correct the
optical path difference (OPD) and optimize aberrations. As
a result, the scanning range in the three-dimensional (3D)
space increased by 15.2%. The ASC method has been proven
to extend the 3D scanning range of RDFD-based scanners
and may have broad application prospects.

Laser three-dimensional (3D) scanning technology, which relies
on the rapid movement of the laser focus in the 3D space, has
found widespread applications in microstructure manufacturing
[1–4], laser cleaning [5–7], and large format texturing [8–11].
The X and Y positions of the laser focus are typically controlled
by two-axis galvanometers [12–16], with scanning speeds of
up to 10 m/s achievable at a working distance of 160 mm [17].
However, achieving comparable scanning speed for the Z posi-
tion of the laser focus remains a challenge. It is a crucial factor
limiting the maximum scanning speed of the laser 3D scanning
system [18]. The scanning speed of the laser focus along the
Z direction, when using a moving concave lens, is three orders
of magnitude slower than that of the X and Y directions [18].
This is because the parallel movement speed of the concave lens
lags far behind the deflection speed of two-axis galvanometers
[19,20]. Methods that manipulate the shape of the lens based on
the principle of electro-wetting [21,22] or shape-variable lenses
[23–26] can rapidly shift the Z direction position of the laser
focus. However, the surface shape of variable lenses is difficult
to control precisely [27]. Reflective dynamic focusing devices
(RDFDs) use two highly dynamic characteristic galvanometers
to convert angular motion into axial motion, increasing the laser
scanning speed in the Z direction [28,29]. However, when shift-
ing the focus Z position, RDFDs need to break the symmetry
of the optical path, which introduces additional optical path dif-
ference (OPD), affecting the focusing quality. Especially when
RDFDs are used in conjunction with an XY scanner for 3D scan-
ning, the XY scanning further amplifies the OPD. This leads to
a decrease in the focusing quality and uniformity in the central
area of the scanning field, and even the edge area cannot be used
for processing. Consequently, the development and application
of RDFDs was limited.
In this Letter, the reasons for the limited scanning range of
RDFD-based 3D laser scanners were discussed. The aberra-
tions caused by the broken symmetry of the optical path were
analyzed. An aspheric symmetry correction (ASC) method was
proposed to optimize OPD and extend the scanning range. An
aspheric correcting lens (CL) was placed in front of the focal
point of the second off-axis parabolic mirror to compensate for
the OPD between the edge rays and the chief ray, and the sym-
metry of the optical path was improved. Etching experiments
were performed to verify the effectiveness of the ASC method.
The optical path of the RDFD-based laser scanner is shown
in Fig. 1(a). The RDFDs consisted of two off-axis parabolic
mirrors (PM1 and PM2) and galvanometers (G1 and G2) placed
at the focal points of PM1 and PM2, respectively. The G1 and
G2 were rotatable. When the focus Z position was not shifted,
the angle between the outgoing and incident beam of G1 and
the angle between the outgoing and incident beam of G2 were
equal to the off-axis angle θ of the parabolic mirrors. The laser-
focusing Z (the direction along the optical axis) position was
controlled by rotating G1 and G2. The rotation angles α of G1
and G2 were equivalent, the rotation directions were opposite,
and the rotation angle of the outgoing beam was ∆θ = 2α. When
G1 and G2 rotated, the primary focus positions between PM1 and
PM2 were shifted forward or backward, as shown by points A1,
B1, and C1 in Fig. 1(a). After reflection by G2, it was scanned
in two dimensions (2D) in the plane perpendicular to the Z
direction by the XY scanner and focused by the F-theta lens
(FL), so secondary focus positions (dynamic focusing positions)
shifted forward or backward, along the Z direction after the FL,
as shown by points A2, B2, and C2 in Fig. 1(a), and 2D scanning
can be performed by XY scanner in the Z plane corresponding
to points A2, B2, and C2. The focus-shift distance ∆Z can be

According to the aberration theory [30] and the Abbe sine
condition [31], there was no coma in the system when the dis-
tance between PM1 and PM2 satisfied that the primary focus
position B1 was located on the symmetry axis of PM1 and PM2.
In this case, the optical path between G1 and G2 was perfectly
symmetric, as shown by the solid blue line, with only a small
amount (−0.017 waves, in our system) of spherical aberration.
During the dynamic focusing process, G1 and G2 deflected (−Z,
green; +Z, red) from the initial angle (Z = 0 mm, blue), and the
symmetrical structure of the optical path was broken by the rota-
tion of G1 and G2. As depicted by the red and green solid lines
shown in the rectangular zoom in Fig. 1(a), the laser entering
the XY scanner was not symmetrical. Consequently, the broken
symmetry resulted in the formation of mainly coma aberrations
at the focal planes FP1 and FP3, affecting the focus quality in
spot diagrams.
Figures 1(c)–1(d) show the variations of OPD with xp/yp and
Z, where xp/yp were the components of the normalized entrance
pupil coordinates in the sagittal/tangential directions with larger
absolute values closer to the edge of rays [30], and Z was the
Z direction dynamic focusing position. As shown in Fig. 1(c),
the absolute values of OPD of the ray in the sagittal direction
increased from the value 0 as the Z position of the focus moved
away from Z = 0 mm to Z = −10 mm/Z = 10 mm. The OPD was
also increased with xp, which indicated that the closer the ray in
the sagittal plane was to the edge, the larger the OPD between
it and the chief ray was. When Z = −10 mm and xp = ±1.0, the
OPD was 0.148 waves, and when Z = 10 mm and xp = ±1, the
OPD was −0.138 waves. The agreement of the values at different
Z planes, for xp = ±1.0, xp = ±0.9, xp = ±0.8, and xp = ±0.7, indi-
cated that this edge effect of rays was symmetric in the sagittal
plane of the beam.
As shown in Fig. 1(d), in the tangential plane, the OPD of the
ray increased to 0.298 waves when Z = −10 mm and yp = −1.0,
and the OPD of the ray was 0.009 waves when Z = −10 mm
and yp = 1.0. The OPD of the ray decreased to −0.269 waves
when Z = 10 mm and yp = -1.0, and the OPD of the ray was -
0.008 waves when Z = 10 mm and yp = 1.0. The disagreements
of the values at different Z planes, for yp = ±1.0, yp = ± 0.9,
yp = ±0.8, and yp = ±0.7 indicated that this edge effect of rays
was asymmetric in the tangential plane of the beam. However,
this asymmetry introduced additional coma aberrations, which
was consistent with the spot diagrams in Fig. 1(a). The asymme-
try of the beam and increased OPD was combined to introduce
aberrations.
The XY scanner scanned the laser-focusing spot in X and
Y directions at each Z plane. However, it acted as an aberra-
tion amplifier, further amplifying the aberration at the center
of each focusing plane [8], so the axial aberration affects the
3D scanning range of the RDFD-based laser scanner. When the
diffraction of the ray was considered, the Strehl ratio (STRH)
reflected the effect of the OPD on the focusing effect in terms
of energy [32]. Therefore, the STRH was used to evaluate the
focusing effect within the scanning range, taking the value of
0 to 1, with closer to 1 indicating better focusing. A STRH
of ≥0.8 is generally considered to correspond to diffraction-
limited performance and effective processing. For STRH of
≤0.3, the behavior of the STRH becomes uncorrelated to the
processing quality due to large aberrations [32]. The STRH,
which was simulated by ZEMAX, within the 3D scanning range
is shown in Fig. 1(b). The optical aperture D was 8 mm, the
laser wavelength λ was 1064 nm, the curvature R of two off-
axis parabolic mirrors was 304.8 mm, the focal length f of
FL was 100 mm, and the distance L of PM1 and PM2 was
410.729 mm. When Z = 0 mm, the STRH was close to 1 within
the scanning range of 40 × 40 mm2. However, when the laser
focus moved backward or forward, the STRH decreased rapidly.
When Z = ±10 mm, the STRH at the corners of the scanning
field decreased to only about 0.3, affecting the effective scanning
range.
An aspheric symmetry correction (ASC) method was pro-
posed to extend the 3D scanning range of RDFD-based laser
scanners. The schematic diagram of the ASC method is shown
in Fig. 2(a). An aspheric correcting lens with a thicker center
and thinner sides was inserted between PM2 and G2 to improve
the symmetry of the optical path to reduce aberrations, and the
initial focused beam (Z = 0 mm, blue) passed through the center
of the lens [31]. The aperture of CL and the distance from CL
to G2 were adjusted so that light within the field of view of G2
could pass through. By adjusting the OPDC (the optical path dif-
ference referenced to the chief ray, expressed in wave number)
and RAED (the angle between the interface normal and the ray
of light after refraction or reflection on the surface, expressed in
degrees) operands in ZEMAX so that the edge rays were opti-
mized towards a symmetric trend when the focusing light path
with asymmetry passes through the lens, the CL can optimize
different focusing light paths in different face shape regions due
to the design of placing the CL between PM2 and G2. As depicted
by red and green solid lines shown in the rectangular zoom in

An experiment was carried out to verify the effectiveness
of the ASC, and the experimental setup is shown in Fig. 3.
A nanosecond fiber laser (YDFLP-100-M8-M-R, JPT) with
a 3.0 mm (expanded to 8 mm, for observing the experimental
results more dramatically) beam diameter was adopted for the
experiment. The wavelength was 1064 nm, the beam quality fac-
tor M2 was 1.4, and the maximum average power was 100 W. The
curvature R of the off-axis parabolic mirrors (35588, Edmund)
was 304.8 mm, and the offset angle was 45°, corresponding to the
initial state of G1. The G1 and G2 can be deflected by ±12.5°. The
scanning speed of the XY scanner (ExtraScan II 14, Hans) was
up to 7 m/s. An F-theta lens with a focal length of 100 mm was
used to focus the laser, and the working distance was 142 mm.
An XYZ stage was placed to move the samples. Two typical
planes of Z = 0 mm and Z = −10 mm were selected to evaluate
the effectiveness of ASC. An aluminum (1060) plate with a size
of 50 × 50 × 1 mm3 was used for experiments, and the laser etch-
ing effect was used as the criterion for evaluating the effective
scanning range. The laser scanning path was a 40 × 40 mm2 rect-
angle, with horizontal and vertical crosshatched lines spaced at
0.5 mm apart. The experimental laser energy was 100 µJ, the
laser frequency was 85 kHz, and the scanning speed was 2 m/s.
A laser scanning confocal microscope (VK-X1100, Keyence)
was used to observe and compare the scanning effect. Table 2
shows the parameters of the CL. It was a positive lens with an
even-ordered aspherical surface at the incidence and a standard
surface at the exit.

At the Z = −10 mm plane, without the ASC method, the STRH
decreases, and the region with STRH of ≥0.8 was only an ellipse
in the 40 × 40 mm2 area based on the STRH of the regional center
of 0.93, as shown in Fig. 4(i). The laser etching result is shown

>0.8 was expanded based on the STRH of the regional center
increasing to 0.99, as shown in Fig. 4(m). The effective laser

show that the ASC method extended the effective scanning range
of RDFD-based laser scanners.
In conclusion, the off-axis aberrations in RDFD-based laser
scanners were presented and discussed. Due to the disruption of
beam symmetry and the increase in the OPD between the edge
and chief ray, the off-axis aberrations were introduced, and the
3D scanning range of RDFD-based laser scanners was reduced.
An ASC method was proposed to optimize the aberration and
extend the scanning range. The convex lens with an aspherical
surface improves the symmetry of the beam and reduces the
OPD between the edge rays and the chief ray. The effectiveness
of the ASC method was confirmed by laser etching experiments,
and the 3D scanning range of the RDFD-based laser scanner has
been increased by 15.2%. The ASC method has the potential to
be applied in all RDFD-based laser scanners or other off-axis
systems.
