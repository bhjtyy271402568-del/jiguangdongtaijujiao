Abstract: Transmissive three-dimensional (3D) laser scanning systems are promising for
precision micro- and nanofabrication, but their scanning range is limited by cumulative optical
path difference (OPD) induced by large-field angular scanning and dynamic axial focusing.
We propose a system-level integrated co-optimization (SLICO) strategy in which the dynamic
focusing module, XY galvanometric scanner, and F-theta telecentric lens are jointly modeled
and optimized for collaborative aberration compensation as a unified system. Simulations
demonstrate that, over an 80 mm diameter XY scanning field and with the Z-axis scanning range
extended from ±10 mm to ±20 mm, the OPD is reduced from 1.0 to 0.25 waves and the Strehl
ratio (STRH) rises from 0.3 to above 0.96. Experimental validation confirms the strategy’s
feasibility and performance. This co-optimization strategy significantly enlarges the scanning
field for a high-quality focal spot and provides a practical solution for high-precision, large-range
laser fabrication.

1.
Introduction:
Laser 3D scanning technology, which relies on the rapid movement of the laser focus in the 3D
space, has found widespread applications in laser material processing [1–4], laser cleaning [5–8],
and 3D printing [9–12]. In such systems, the coupled operation of the dynamic focusing module,
XY galvanometer scanner, and F-theta telecentric lens plays a decisive role in determining
the focal spot quality and the effective scanning range, thereby directly influencing the overall
processing efficiency and precision. However, in transmissive 3D laser scanning systems, these
optical components are generally designed and optimized independently, with each module
minimizing its own optical path difference (OPD). When the modules are integrated into a
complete system, optical coupling among the subsystems introduces additional system-level
aberrations and OPD that are not accounted for in the separate designs. Moreover, the lateral
beam deflection induced by the XY galvanometer scanner further amplifies the OPD across the
scanning field. As a result, the focal spot quality progressively degrades along both the axial
and lateral directions. This degradation becomes particularly pronounced over large scanning
volumes, where the STRH, focal spot circularity and diameter deteriorate significantly, leading to
reduced processing uniformity and a restricted scanning range.
In response to the growing demand for high-speed and high-precision 3D laser processing in
both industrial and scientific applications, various advanced Z-axis dynamic focusing elements
have been developed, including variable-focus liquid lenses [13–18], tunable acoustic gradient-
index (TAG) lenses [19–22], and deformable mirrors [23–26]. These devices offer advantages

in terms of response speed, focal tunability, and system compactness. Despite these advances,

and focal spot distortions [27,28] remain significant challenges. In addition, reflective dynamic
focusing devices achieve 3D laser processing by converting galvanometer angular motion into
axial displacement [29–31]. However, such systems typically require four galvanometer scanners,
which increases system cost and complexity while restricting the achievable 3D scanning range.
As a result, achieving uniform and high-quality focal spots over an extended 3D scanning range
remains challenging for existing dynamic focusing schemes, thereby limiting the feasibility
of large-area, high-precision 3D laser processing and motivating further investigation into
transmissive 3D laser scanning systems. In contrast, studies on transmissive 3D laser scanning
systems remain relatively limited, and the interplay between system configuration and aberration
remains insufficiently understood with a lack of systematic design methodologies.
In this paper, the mechanisms that limit the scanning range in transmissive 3D laser scanning
systems are systematically investigated. A system-level integrated co-optimization (SLICO)
strategy is proposed, in which the dynamic focusing module, XY galvanometer scanner, and
F-theta telecentric lens are jointly optimized to suppress system-level OPD and expand the
usable 3D scanning range. A comprehensive simulation model is developed to predict the
evolution of the STRH, focal spot circularity and diameter throughout the entire scanning space.
Experimental validation is subsequently performed. The results demonstrate that the proposed
strategy maintains uniform, high-quality focal spots across the full 3D scanning range, thereby
significantly improving processing consistency and reliability.

As indicated in Eq. (2), the system focal position P(x,y,z) is determined jointly by the deflection
angles of the XY galvanometer scanner and the divergence angle of the output beam emitted from
the dynamic focusing module. The dynamic focusing module is actuated by a voice coil motor

scanner. This configuration ensures high speed, synchronized 3D focusing. Because both the
galvanometer deflection angles and the output beam divergence can be precisely controlled via
input voltages, the laser focal spot can be scanned along a predetermined trajectory in 3D space
by appropriately designing the voltage drive functions, while maintaining the system’s dynamic
performance under high-speed operation.

scalar wave. The electric field distribution E(r, t) at the entrance pupil is governed by the scalar
wave equation:
E(r, t) = A(r)exp[i(k · r −ωt + ϕ)]

where A(r) is the amplitude, k is the wave vector, w is the angular frequency, and φ represents the

transmissive 3D laser scanning systems, the aberration correction and the design of focal-plane
flatness for the F-theta telecentric lens are typically performed under the assumption that the
beam incident on the entrance pupil constitutes an ideal plane wave, where the wavefront function

Under this condition, the phase distribution across the aperture is uniform, allowing system

However, the introduction of the dynamic focusing module induces a dynamic divergence

As a result, the pre-designed correction of the F-theta telecentric lens becomes ineffective, leading
to focal spot distortion, increased spot diameter, and degraded 3D scanning performance.
2.2.
Simulation and analysis of the conventional transmissive 3D laser scanning
system
Based on the theoretical analysis, a conventional transmissive 3D laser scanning model with direct
integration was constructed to further investigate the underlying mechanisms responsible for the
observed performance degradation. To establish a rigorous comparative baseline in the optical
simulations, the conventional 3D scanning system was modeled using an equivalent optical
approach. Because the exact internal prescriptions of the commercial LINOS F-theta lens and the
Scanlab dynamic focusing unit are proprietary, an equivalent F-theta lens model matching the
macroscopic specifications of the physical setup (focal length f = 167 mm, operating wavelength
λ = 1064 nm) was utilized. Furthermore, the conventional dynamic focusing module was modeled
as an aberration-free paraxial surface with a variable focal length to represent the ideal Z-axis shift.
During the multi-configuration optimization for the 3D scanning volume, the focal length of this
paraxial surface was dynamically adjusted within the ranges of (-∞, -1250 mm] and [1450 mm,
∞). This parameter variation precisely corresponds to a physical working distance shift from
196.660 mm to 236.367 mm (centered at 216.342 mm), effectively covering the ± 20 mm Z-axis
scanning range. In this model, the dynamic focusing module is idealized as lenses without
intrinsic aberrations and is directly integrated with the XY galvanometer scanner and the F-theta
telecentric lens. System performance was evaluated via numerical simulations using the Physical
Optics Propagation (POP) method with a Gaussian input beam. The simulations focused on the
OPD and focusing quality across different Z-axis planes and galvanometer deflection angles. By
adjusting the focal length of the dynamic focusing module, a Z-axis dynamic focusing range
of ±20 mm was achieved. The normalized pupil coordinates along the X and Y directions are
denoted by xp and yp, respectively. The simulation results are presented in Fig. 2. Figure 2(a)-(b)
illustrate the OPD distributions at different Z-axis positions for the on-axis condition, where (θx,
θy) = (0, 0) and the corresponding scan-field coordinate is (x, y) = (0 mm, 0 mm). As the Z-axis
position shifts from Z = 0 mm to Z = ±20 mm, the absolute OPD along the xp and yp directions
increase monotonically, indicating that larger Z-axis offsets induce greater wavefront deviation,
particularly for marginal rays. The OPD profiles along the two pupil directions remain nearly
identical at each Z plane, consistent with the system’s inherent axial symmetry. Under off-axis
field conditions, the OPD becomes markedly asymmetric. For a galvanometer deflection of (θx,
θy) = (0, 7), corresponding to (x, y)= (0 mm, 40 mm), the resulting OPD distributions shown in
Fig. 2(c)-(d) reveal that the OPD along the X direction stays below 0.25 waves, whereas the OPD
along the Y direction increases significantly with both yp and Z-axis displacement. A comparable
but orthogonally oriented trend is observed for (θx, θy) = (7, 0), corresponding to (x, y) = (40 mm,
0 mm), as illustrated in Fig. 2(e)-(f): the OPD along Y remains within 0.25 waves, while the
OPD along X increases substantially with increasing xp and Z-axis offset.
Overall, the results in Fig. 2(a)-(f) demonstrate a clear dependence of OPD on both galvanometer
deflection and axial focusing position. Within the Z-axis range of |Z| ≤10 mm, the OPD remains
below 0.5 waves across the full scan field, indicating stable wavefront quality. Beyond this range,

different Z-axis planes and galvanometer deflection conditions, the STRH, focal spot diameter
and circularity were employed as evaluation metrics. The simulation results are presented in
Fig. 3(a)-(c). At the focal plane Z = 0 mm, the system exhibits near diffraction limited performance

scanning field. When the focal plane is offset to Z = ±10 mm, the focusing performance begins to
degrade: the STRH decreases to approximately 0.8, the focal-spot circularity drops to 80%, and
the spot diameter increases, leading to reduced focal-spot uniformity. Further displacement to
Z = ±20 mm results in more pronounced degradation: the STRH falls below 0.8, the circularity
decreases to approximately 40%, and the focal-spot diameter reaches a maximum of 80 µm,
accompanied by a significant reduction in spot uniformity. Under these conditions, the system is
no longer suitable for high-precision laser processing.

Beyond this range, system aberrations increase significantly, leading to degradation of the focal
spot quality. This limitation arises from the optical design of dynamic focusing module and the
F-theta telecentric lens, which are generally designed and optimized independently. During the
individual optimization stage, each module minimizes its own OPD under nominal operating
conditions. When these modules are integrated into a complete system, optical coupling among
the subsystems is neglected, and additional system-level aberrations and OPD are introduced
that are not accounted for in the separate designs. During 3D laser scanning, the dynamic
focusing module inherently modifies the beam divergence, deviating from the parallel-beam
condition required by the F-theta telecentric lens and thereby introducing additional aberrations.
Moreover, off-axis beam deflection induced by the XY galvanometer scanner further amplifies
the OPD across the scanning field. Under such conditions, the aberrations originating from the
dynamic focusing module and the F-theta telecentric lens cannot be effectively compensated.
As a result, the focal spot quality progressively degrades along both the axial and lateral
directions. This degradation becomes particularly pronounced over large scanning volumes,
where key spot metrics—including the STRH, focal spot circularity and diameter—deteriorate
significantly. Consequently, the processing uniformity is reduced, the high-quality focusing range
is substantially narrowed, and the achievable 3D scanning range of the system is severely limited.
2.3.
Simulation and analysis of the system-level integrated co-optimized transmissive
3D laser scanning system
To overcome this limitation and further extend the scanning range of the transmissive 3D laser
scanning system, a SLICO strategy is proposed. In this strategy, the dynamic focusing module,
XY galvanometer scanner, and F-theta telecentric lens are treated as a single, unified optical
subsystem. Through multi-parameter coordinated design, system-level aberration balance is
achieved, resulting in a fundamental improvement in focal-spot quality during 3D scanning. The
systematic workflow of this proposed strategy is detailed in the algorithm flowchart presented in
Fig. 4. As illustrated, the co-optimization process is fundamentally conducted in two stages: In
the first stage, the dynamic focusing module and the F-theta telecentric lens are independently
designed and preliminarily optimized. By adjusting parameters such as lens curvature, element
thickness, optical material, and inter-element spacing, each module is optimized to minimize
standalone aberrations. In the second stage, a SLICO model encompassing all three components
is constructed, and system-level iterative optimization is conducted to achieve coordinated
performance enhancement across the complete optical path. During this process, evaluation
functions that simultaneously consider multiple Z-axis positions and a range of galvanometer
deflection angles are introduced. Key performance metrics, including RMS wavefront error,
STRH, and spot circularity, are simultaneously constrained. To achieve a system-level aberration
balance, the SLICO strategy transitions to a co-optimization stage driven by a composite merit
function (MF) within a multi-configuration environment. The MF is defined as:

The SLICO strategy significantly enhances the overall optical performance of the system.
As shown in Fig. 5(a)-(f), within a circular XY scanning field of 80 mm in diameter and a
Z-axis focusing range of ±20 mm, the optimized system maintains the OPD below 0.25 waves,
demonstrating a substantial improvement in wavefront quality. Further analysis of the focal
spot metrics, presented in Fig. 6(a)–(c), shows that across this extended 3D scanning range, the
STRH remains above 0.96, the focal-spot diameter is stabilized approximately 33.5 µm, and the
focal-spot circularity exceeds 98%, closely approaching an ideal circular shape. These results
confirm that the focal spot quality is significantly improved compared with the pre-optimized
system. Overall, the simulation results verify that the proposed SLICO methodology effectively
mitigates aberrations induced by dynamic focusing and XY galvanometer deflection. As a
result, the system is capable of maintaining near-diffraction-limited focal performance over a
large-area XY scan and extended Z-axis excursions. Compared with the conventional, directly
integrated transmissive 3D dynamic focusing system, the optimized design doubles the Z-axis
scanning range from ±10 mm to ±20 mm. This improvement demonstrates the effectiveness of
the proposed approach in enhancing optical design robustness, improving focusing performance,
and expanding the 3D laser processing capability.

strategy, a quantitative breakdown of the specific aberrations was performed. Initially, the
wavefront errors of the systems were decomposed using standard Zernike polynomials. To
provide the most intuitive physical insight into the asymmetric optical paths caused by the
inherent physical separation of the X and Y galvanometer mirrors, these low-order Zernike terms
were directly mapped to their corresponding Seidel aberrations. These primary aberrations
were systematically evaluated at the maximum deflection fields along both orthogonal axes: (θx,
θy) = (0, 7) and (7, 0) under extreme Z-axis shifts (Z = ± 20 mm). The quantitative breakdown is
presented in Table 1.
In the conventional integrated system, dynamically adjusting the working distance inherently
breaks the aberration balance of the commercial F-theta lens. Off-axis aberrations dominate
at the scanning boundaries, particularly astigmatism and field curvature. For example, at (θx,
θy) = (0, 7) with Z = 20 mm, the astigmatism coefficient for the conventional system reaches
a substantial value of 0.2309, accompanied by a field curvature of 0.0914. These prominent
asymmetrical aberrations inevitably lead to severe focal spot distortion, directly explaining the
drastic drop in spot circularity observed in the previous sections.

These comprehensive physical simulations conclusively prove that the SLICO strategy ensures
robust, high-resolution focusing quality and stable spatial frequency response throughout the
entire extended 3D scanning volume. Based on these theoretical and simulated validations,
the subsequent section presents the experimental implementation to verify the actual physical

Experimental validation and comparative analysis
Comparative experiments were carried out to verify the effectiveness of the optimized system,
and a laser scanning platform incorporating both the conventional directly integrated system
and the SLICO transmissive 3D laser scanning system was established, as shown in Fig. 9. A
picosecond fiber laser (YDFLP-E2-20-M7-S-R, JPT) with a wavelength of 1064 nm, a beam
quality factor of M2 = 1.4 and a maximum average output power of 20 W was employed. To
enable a rigorous comparison of scanning dynamics and address the hardware kinematic limits,
the specific hardware configurations of both systems are detailed in Table 2. The X- and Y-axis
galvanometers, G1 and G2, were configured with maximum deflection angles of ±7° for both
systems. The focal spot diameter was experimentally determined by analyzing the laser marking
results on the sample surface. A Keyence Digital Microscopy System (VHX series) was utilized
to capture high-resolution images of the ablation spots and measure their effective diameters.
Throughout the measurement trials, the laser power and scanning speed were maintained strictly
constant. Consequently, the measured diameter, which is defined by the boundary where the laser
intensity exceeds the material’s ablation threshold, corresponds closely to the 1/e2 beam width.
The reported data points represent the arithmetic average of these 3 independent measurements,
with the measurement uncertainty expressed as error bars denoting ± 1 standard deviation.
Three representative axial planes (Z = 0 mm and Z = ±20 mm) were selected for evaluation. For
each plane, four circular scanning paths corresponding to 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, and 1.0 times the full
scanning field were defined, with diameters of Φ=24 mm, Φ=40 mm, Φ=56 mm, and Φ=80 mm,
respectively. All scans were performed on a conductive-glass substrate (100 × 100 × 1.1 mm3).
The resulting laser-etched patterns were observed using a laser scanning confocal microscope to
quantitatively assess the focal-spot quality, effective scanning range, and scanning stability of
both systems.

4.
Conclusion
In conclusion, this study systematically analyzed the 3D scanning mechanism of a transmissive
3D laser scanning system The analysis focused on focal-spot degradation and optical aberrations
introduced by the dynamic focusing module, the XY galvanometer scanner, and the F-theta
telecentric lens under large-field scanning and Z-axis refocusing conditions. To mitigate the
additional system-level aberrations and the limited 3D scanning range caused by large-field
angular scanning and dynamic axial focusing, a SLICO strategy was developed, coordinating
all optical modules. This method reduces the OPD from 1.0 waves to 0.25 waves, increases the
STRH from 0.3 to above 0.96, and stabilizes the focal spot diameter across the 3D scanning
range. Experimental results verify that the average circularity of laser-etched spots is ≥73.5%,
the relative fluctuation of spot diameter is below 1%, and the effective Z-axis scanning range is
doubled from ±10 mm to ±20 mm within a circular XY scanning field of 80 mm in diameter.
The results demonstrate that the SLICO strategy effectively suppresses aberrations, maintains
high focal spot quality and expands the 3D scanning range. This work not only provides a
generalizable methodology for high-precision, large-field 3D laser scanning systems but also
offers a practical engineering solution for precision micro- and nanofabrication.
