A B S T R A C T
Micro-engineered transparent flexible polymers components play a crucial role in various microsystem fields, 
such as flexible electronics and microfluidics. However, conventional laser fabrication techniques face significant 
challenges in overcoming issues of energy deposition inaccuracies and focal mismatch, which hinder the fabri­
cation of high-fidelity and controllable 3D microstructure in transparent polymer materials. In this study, we 
propose a universal 3D dynamic-focusing laser (3D-DFL) fabrication strategy using an infrared (IR) picosecond 
laser. By dynamically adjusting the Z-axis focus in real time, the system effectively compensates for the depth 
shifts caused by ablation, ensuring consistent energy deposition and stable fabrication quality. High-speed im­
aging reveals a three-stage ablation mechanism (stabilization, expansion, and contraction) under laser irradia­
tion. To support the multi-layer dynamic shifting process of the 3D-DFL approach, a universal ablation depth 
prediction model was established to compensate depth deviations during laser-material interactions. The validity 
of the model has been proven by its ability to predict ablation depth in different polymer materials with low 
mean absolute percentage errors (MAPE), achieving 5.99 % for polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) and 2.68 % for 
polyethylene terephthalate (PET). The model enables the accurate fabrication of 3D microstructures, achieving 
normalized peak-to-valley deviations within 8.0 % and normalized root-mean-square deviations below 3.0 %, 
with an arithmetic surface roughness of approximately 2 μm. The 3D dynamic-focusing laser (3D-DFL) approach 
enables rapid tailoring of complex geometries, including protruding and recessed microstructures on PDMS and 
PET substrates. Experimental validation highlights its capability to fabricate functional components such as 
flexible pressure sensors, microfluidic chips, and ultrasonic droplet manipulation platforms. This study provides 
an efficient and reliable pathway for the scalable fabricating of high-precision transparent polymers micro- 
engineered devices and promotes the advancement of research and industry in advanced flexible microsystems.

three-dimensional (3D) microstructures can significantly enhance their

has become the most widely used method for microengineering trans­
parent flexible polymer (name as template method). In this approach, a 
master mold is first created and then used to cast polymer structures 
repeatedly [11]. This method provides high precision and scalability. 
However, its inherently complex workflow, which includes mold fabri­
cation, casting, curing, demolding, and assembly, leads to high pro­
duction costs, long fabrication cycles, and strong dependence on manual 
operations [2]. Additionally, structural fractures and dimensional dis­
tortions may occur when producing high aspect ratios or complex 
structures. These drawbacks limit its effectiveness for customized 
fabrication and rapid design iterations, significantly increasing both 
time and cost [12]. In addition, several alternative microengineering 
methods have been developed to overcome the limitations of replica 
molding. Extrusion-based 3D printing, for example, enables direct 
microengineering on flexible substrates but suffers from low resolution 
(over 100 μm) and poor shape fidelity [13]. Two-photon polymerization 
achieves submicrometer resolution, but its use in polymer structuring is 
constrained by photocuring limitations and high resin viscosity, limiting 
the effective fabricating area to under 200 μm [14]. Despite their po­
tential, these methods still have trade-offs between resolution, scal­
ability, and material compatibility. Therefore, developing a direct 
polymer microengineering technology that eliminates multi-step mold­
ing and enables on-demand, efficient fabrication of 3D microstructures 
is a critical breakthrough for meeting the growing demand for trans­
parent flexible polymer-based devices.
Laser technology has become widely used for fabricating 3D micro­
structures due to its non-contact, high precision, efficiency, and cus­
tomizability. This technique shows significant potential in various 
materials and applications [15]. Traditional laser microengineering uses 
a fixed-focus system, allowing 3D microstructures to be created by 
controlling the local energy input. Axinte et al. [16,17] controlled the 
energy beam using a dwell-time strategy to regulate local energy input. 
Specifically, they employed a removal rate function to adjust the scan­
ning speed between different dwell points. By optimizing the dwell time, 
over ablation was avoided, and the method was successfully applied to 
fabricate 3D structures in materials such as graphite and titanium alloys, 
achieving precise single-layer material removal. However, the 
dwell-time strategy requires machines with high acceleration and 
deceleration capabilities [16], and excessive acceleration (i.e., abrupt 
speed changes) may lead to mechanical vibrations and motion errors. 
Cheng et al. [18] proposed a programmable method for fabricating 
microstructures on PMMA by applying regional ablation to fabricate 
pyramid feature. However, the pyramid morphology on transparent 
materials was found to be unclear and inconsistent. This issue arises 
because when using a fixed focal plane for laser fabricating, an increase 
in ablation depth leads to a mismatch between the laser focus and the 
material surface, resulting in laser defocusing. This defocusing is one of 
the main factors contributing to reduced fabricating accuracy. Although 
local energy control (dwell-time) strategies can enable microstructure 
fabrication, they have several limitations: (1) energy control becomes 
significantly more complex for intricate structures; (2) performance may 
be limited when fabricating sharp edges and high-aspect-ratio features; 
and (3) mismatches between the laser focus and ablation depth are 
prone to occur. Tu et al. [19] demonstrated that when the laser focus 
depth is shallow, even slight defocusing can cause the workpiece surface 
to fall outside the effective laser range, resulting in inaccurate energy 
deposition and uncontrolled ablation. If the focal point is located 
beneath the material surface, it may induce subsurface effects such as 
internal bubble formation and structural distortion [20]. Florian et al. 
[21] pointed out that when the laser focus is positioned above the 
sample surface, the resulting structures tend to exhibit irregular de­
formations. These issues significantly reduce both the geometric accu­
racy and surface quality of the structures, especially in transparent or 
semi-transparent materials. This highlights the critical importance of 
precise focus alignment in the laser fabrication process, as such prob­
lems cannot be effectively addressed by merely adjusting local energy 
input or dwell time. Therefore, overcoming the challenges of (1) com­
plex energy control and (2) abnormal energy deposition caused by 
defocusing has become a critical issue in the laser fabrication of complex 
3D microstructures.
To address the above issues, researchers have explored more 
advanced 3D laser fabrication techniques. These techniques aim to 
achieve precise energy deposition, simplify energy control, and effec­
tively reduce subsurface effects, thereby improving the quality of 
transparent polymer fabrication. Currently, two primary approaches are 
used for 3D laser fabrication. The first approach combines a two- 
dimensional (2D) galvanometer with a high-precision multi-axis mo­
tion stage, where spatial positioning is achieved by moving either the 
workpiece or the laser head [22,23]. This approach has a simple struc­
ture, with stage positioning accuracy reaching sub-micron levels [24]. 
However, it faces several limitations: (1) the response speed is relatively 
slow, each adjustment requires several milliseconds for acceleration, 
positioning, and deceleration, with a focusing frequency below 100 Hz; 
(2) the system has high inertia, making it not well suited for high-speed 
or continuous focusing applications; (3) there is a trade-off between 
motion speed and accuracy, higher speeds typically reduce accuracy, the 
motion speed is usually limited to below 500 mm/s to maintain accu­
racy; and (4) coordination between the motion stage and the laser sys­
tem is complex, and frequent displacements may introduce positioning 
errors and cumulative inaccuracies, thus reducing processing precision. 
In comparison, the second approach uses an optical system to adjust the 
focal position along the Z-axis. This method offers a fast response time in 
the sub-millisecond range, with modulation frequency exceeding 1 kHz 
and micron-level focusing accuracy. It enables real-time focal depth 
adjustment during high-speed scanning and significantly reduces delays 
from mechanical motion. Under the same vertical motion precision, its 
effective Z-axis focusing speed is approximately three orders of magni­
tude higher than that of a mechanical motion stage [26]. This makes it 
particularly suitable for fabricating continuous curved surfaces, ramp 
structures, and complex 3D profiles with significant depth variation. In 
addition to improved fabricating efficiency, the optical system also of­
fers higher performance in terms of fabrication accuracy, consistency, 
and system stability.
Recently, various optical focusing techniques have been explored to 
meet the demands of high-speed, high-precision focal adjustment. Du 
et al. [25] used a tunable acoustic gradient (TAG) index lens driven by 
cylindrical piezoelectric ceramics to achieve high-speed focal point 
oscillation at kHz-MHz frequencies. The compact system does not 
require complex synchronization with the laser. However, although it 
enables rapid Z-axis focusing, it still requires integration with an XY 
motion stage for full 3D spatial control, which presents challenges. 
Additionally, controlling lens curvature remains difficult [26]. Cao et al. 
[27] achieved dynamic focusing by moving a negative lens in combi­
nation with Y-axis sample movement, enabling depth-adjustable laser 
ablation that enhanced both efficiency and fabrication quality. Zhang 
et al. [28] used a dual parabolic mirror and galvanometer to adjust the 
divergence angle of incident beam, achieving 3-axis focusing control 
with focus an 80 mm focus-shifting range. However, the optical path

Overall, although 3D laser fabrication technologies have made sig­

urgent need for simpler, more efficient, and adaptable fabrication stra­

laser fabrication of 3D microstructures in transparent polymers, espe­

understood, and the potential of dynamic focusing to enhance the pre­

high-fidelity and controllable fabrication of 3D microstructures in 
transparent polymer materials using laser-based approaches, while 
overcoming common issues such as energy deposition inaccuracy and 
focal mismatch (Fig. 1). To overcome this challenge, we propose a 
universal 3D dynamic focusing laser (3D-DFL) fabrication strategy for 
transparent flexible polymers. This strategy adjusts the focal point in 
real-time, effectively compensating for the depth shift, ensuring 
consistent energy deposition and fabrication quality. To support the 3D- 
DFL process, we established an ablation depth prediction model that 
simulates ablation depths under various laser parameters. The model 
corrects focal mismatch issues and addresses depth deviations in laser- 
material interactions, which is critical for transparent polymers where 
energy penetration changes non-linearly with depth. The model has no 
inherent limitations on the materials or lasers used; it only requires a few 
experiments to determine the ablation threshold of material and ab­
sorption coefficients for different wavelengths, enabling accurate 
predictions.

2. Methodology
2.1. 3D dynamic-focusing laser fabricating system setup
The experimental setup for the 3D-DFL fabricating system is shown 
in Fig. 2. The system uses an infrared picosecond laser (PINE-1064-20, 
China) with a maximum output power of 20 W, a wavelength of 1064 
nm, a repetition rate of 100 kHz, and a pulse width of 10 ps. A beam 
quality factor (M2) of 1.3. The laser beam is expanded by a standard 
beam expander to reduce the divergence angle and improve beam 
collimation before it enters the focusing optics. After beam expansion, 
the input beam diameter is 7.2 mm. Then, the beam is directed to the Z- 
axis adjustment module via a series of high-reflection mirrors, followed 
by the XY scanning module, and finally focused onto the sample surface 
by the F-Theta focusing lens (focal length: 160 mm).
The basic working principle of the dynamic focusing system is shown 
in Fig. 2b. The system consists of a concave-convex lens pair in the Z-axis 
focusing module. The laser beam first passes through the concave lens 
group, where it is diverged, and then through the convex lens group to 
converge, before entering the XY galvanometer scanner and the field 
lens. The core of the Z-axis focusing module is to control the axial (Z 
direction) displacement of the final focusing position by adjusting the 
divergence angle and beam diameter of the incident laser beam. Spe­
cifically, the Z-axis focusing module includes adjustable optical com­
ponents (such as movable lens groups or adaptive optical elements), 
which can move along the optical axis to alter the divergence of the 
output beam. When the focusing module moves toward the field lens, 
the divergence angle of the incident beam increases, causing the focus to 
move forward (closer to the field lens); conversely, when the module 
moves away, the focus shifts backward (further from the field lens). This 
small change in divergence angle is converted into axial displacement of 
the focus point by the field lens, thus achieving non-contact dynamic 
focusing without moving the sample.
Before entering the field lens, the laser beam is deflected along the X 
and Y directions by two high-speed scanning mirrors. This design allows 
the laser beam to perform two-dimensional dynamic deflection near the 
optical axis, enabling rapid pattern drawing. The beam finally enters the 
F-theta lens, which is composed of multiple optimized spherical or 
aspherical lens. This lens corrects aberrations and compensates for focal

2.2. 2D laser and 3D dynamic-focusing laser fabricating method
The subtractive fabricating process for three-dimensional (3D) mi­
crostructures typically employs a layer-by-layer material removal 
strategy. As shown in Fig. 3a, the working principle of the laser-based 
method for fabricating 3D microstructure arrays involves sequentially 
removing material from the substrate in a top-down manner. After each 
layer is fabricated, the laser focal spot is shifted downward to machine 
the subsequent layer. This approach is akin to sculpting, where each 
successive layer is precisely removed until the desired 3D architecture is 
formed.
2.2.1. 2D laser fabricating method
Different scanning strategies have a considerable impact on micro­
structure formation. Traditional laser fabricating (2D laser) uses a fixed- 
focus system, where the laser focal point is maintained at a constant 
height, the laser energy is concentrated near the focal region, while the 
defocused zones on both sides of the focal plane exhibit a rapid decrease 
in energy density due to the Gaussian distribution of the laser. There­
fore, removing microstructures at different depths relies on precise 
control of laser energy (local energy control strategy), as shown in 
Fig. 3b. This energy attenuation, coupled with thermal effects in the 
defocused regions, reduces fabrication precision.
Fig. 3c shows the schematic of the scanning trajectory. By 
segmenting the scanning area and controlling the laser energy distri­
bution, the designed 3D microstructure can be achieved. The laser en­
ergy density is gradually increased from the central region to the outer 
region, removing more material toward the periphery and yielding a 
shape with a higher center and lower edges. This fabrication process 
requires a complex spatial energy modulation strategy to attain specific 
ablation depths in designated regions. However, the 2D laser approach 
faces a fundamental trade-off in segmentation resolution: coarser 
regional divisions lead to terraced-edge structures, whereas finer di­
visions significantly increase the complexity of ablation depth control. 
Consequently, achieving smooth transitional interfaces becomes 
considerably more difficult.
To fabricate tapered microstructure, the substrate was segmented 
into five distinct regions (Fig. A1), with a total of five layers fabricated. 
We performed extensive trial-and-error optimization of each layer 
parameter in the 2D laser fabricating experiments, including variables 
such as scanning speed, laser power, and scanning times. The laser 
scanning speed and number of scans for each region were individually 
regulated, with detail fabricating parameters provided in Table A1. The 
resulting tapered microstructure fabricated by the 2D laser method ex­
hibits a terraced-edge structure, as shown in Fig. 3c. The scanning 
electron microscope (SEM) side-view in Fig. 3d further confirms the

2.2.2. 3D dynamic-focusing laser fabricating method
In contrast, Fig. 3e shows the 3D dynamic focusing and real-time 
laser focal plane shifting mechanism. During fabrication, the galvo 
system controls the laser beam’s motion along a predefined trajectory, 
while the Z-axis dynamic focusing module simultaneously adjusts the 
focal plane height (range: ±10 mm) to compensate for defocusing ef­
fects induced by material surface topography variations. This synchro­
nized approach ensures that as the focal point dynamically shifts 
downward, the energy density delivered to the sample surface remains 
consistent, a critical advantage over conventional 2D laser processing. 
By integrating high-speed XY-plane scanning with adaptive Z-axis ad­
justments, this method achieves layer-by-layer (a total of 15 layers) 
fabrication of complex 3D microstructures, as shown in Fig. 3f.
Notably, tapered microstructures fabricated by 3D-DFL method 
exhibit no pronounced terraced-edge morphology, in stark contrast to 
their 2D-laser-processed counterparts. This improvement arises from the 
real-time downward shifting of the laser focal point after each 
completed layer, which adapts to the evolving fabricating plane height 
and sustains optimal laser energy distribution. Such dynamic focal 
control eliminates the energy density gradients inherent to fixed-plane 
2D laser method, where Gaussian beam decay along the Z-axis leads 
to non-uniform ablation and thermal accumulation. Consequently, as 
the number of fabricated layers increases, the 3D profile contours pro­
gressively smooth and sharp (Fig. 3f), demonstrating enhanced geo­
metric fidelity. Precise regulation of the laser’s dynamic shifting 
distance is thus pivotal to minimizing edge roughness and enhancing 
accuracy. The SEM image shown in Fig. 3g further validates the supe­
riority of 3D-DFL, showcasing sharp defect-free edges and geometric 
consistency.
2.2.3. Comparison between 2D laser and 3D laser methods
The experiments show that, although we thoroughly optimized the 
laser process parameters in the 2D laser fabricating experiment and 
achieved microstructure removal at different depths by precisely con­
trolling the laser energy (local energy control strategy), including 
scanning speed, power, and scanning times, the process remains time- 
consuming. However, the pyramid microstructures fabricated still 
show terraced-edge structure. For sharp or complex structures, the 
limitations of the fixed-focus 2D laser system in controlling the focal 
point led to a slow and costly process, as well as issues like complex 
ablation control and uneven structure morphology. In contrast, the 3D

2.3. Sample preparation and characterization
The PDMS used in this study (Sylgard 184) was purchased from Dow 
Corning, USA. The preparation process of PDMS commenced with the 
mixing of the prepolymer and curing agent in a mass ratio of 10:1, fol­
lowed by thorough mixing for 20 min. To remove air bubbles from the 
mixture, it was then placed in a vacuum drying oven for evacuation. 
Subsequently, the mixture was poured into molds and cured at a con­
stant temperature of 80 ◦C for 2 h. Finally, the PDMS film was peeled off 
from the molds.
The samples were observed with a scanning electron microscope 
(SEM) instrument (SUPRA55 SAPPHIRE, Zeiss Corporation, Germany) 
equipped with an energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS) was used to 
characterize the structure and morphology of the microstructure. The 
3D spatial morphology of the microstructures was characterized by 3D 
laser confocal microscopy (VK-X1000, Keyence, Japan). The elemental 
analysis of the sample was performed using an X-ray Photoelectron 
Spectroscopy (XPS) instrument (Thermo Scientific K-Alpha, USA) 
equipped with an Al K alpha (1486.6 eV) X-ray source.

through a photothermal ablation mechanism mediated by nonlinear 
processes, such as multiphoton absorption [35].

synchronized with a high-power pulsed illumination system (Cavitar HF

pulsed illumination, enabling precise visualization of transient high- 
temperature phenomena. The Phantom camera operated at a 
maximum frame rate of 320,000 fps, significantly exceeding the laser 
repetition rate (100 kHz), as shown in Fig. 4b.

material expansion. Subsequent laser pulses further increase surface 
temperature until surpassing the ablation threshold, triggering instan­

mechanism facilitates the detachment of atomic/molecular clusters

material removal pathway. The contraction stage occurs after the laser 
pulse ceases. The surface heat source disappears, and the ablated region

rapid pressure drop, releasing a strong inward compression wave [37].

internal support. Following the contraction, the material enters the

3.2.1. Bottom surface morphology analysis
To evaluate the influence of focal conditions on surface quality, a 
series of experiments were conducted under negative defocus (−500 μm, 
−250 μm), in-focus, and positive defocus (+500 μm, +250 μm) condi­
tions, with laser laser powers ranging from 2.81 W to 3.96 W. Three 
samples were fabricated for each parameter set, and the corresponding 
surface morphology and arithmetic surface roughness (Ra) results of the 
CDEF plane are shown in Fig. 6.
At a laser power of 2.81 W, insufficient laser energy under defocused 
condition resulted in non-uniform ablation, producing coarse particles 
on surface. These particles were randomly distributed and exhibited 
smooth surfaces, indicating the presence of partially ablated PDMS 
residues. Increasing the power to 3.79 W reduced incomplete ablation, 
resulting in finer particle and reducing arithmetic surface roughness 
(Ra) from 5 μm to 2 μm (Fig. 6b). However, further increasing the laser 
power to 4.45 W did not further reduce surface roughness; instead, a 
slight increase in roughness was observed (Fig. A2). At this higher 
power, the heat delivered per unit area exceeded the material’s optimal 
processing window, leading to over-ablation. This is attributed to the 
excessive heat input exceeding the material’s optimal fabricating 
threshold, leading to over-ablation. The high energy density likely 
triggered phase explosion phenomena, causing the redeposition of 
molten debris and consequently increasing surface roughness. Addi­
tionally, at a power of 2.3 W and a negative defocus of 700 μm, signif­
icant subsurface bubble-like ablative structures were observed within 
the PDMS (Fig. A3), exhibiting morphologies distinctly different from 
those produced under in-focus conditions.
Overall, the experimental results indicate that in-focus fabrication 
consistently achieves lower surface roughness compared to defocused 
conditions. This further highlights the importance of accurate focus 
alignment for high-precision microfabrication.

3.2.3. Sidewall elemental analysis
The primary elements of PDMS are carbon (C), oxygen (O), and sil­

converted to CO2 or CO and volatilized, reducing the residual carbon. In 
contrast, under positive or negative defocus conditions, lower power 
density weakens ablation, leading to incomplete carbon removal and 
enrichment of residual carbonaceous organics, which increases the

Fig. 9 shows the average ablation depth for fabricating two to six

When fabricating relatively shallow structures only a few layers are 
required (for example, less than six layers), and the advantage of focus 
adjustment may not be very pronounced. However, such feature struc­

approach is specifically designed for deeper structures (200–500 μm) 
such as micro-channels, stepped features, and inclined surfaces, more 
than 10–30 fabricating layers are required to obtain a smooth and 
continuous feature profile.

scanning line spacing of 6–8 μm, and a scanning speed of 700–800 mm/s 
typically results in an ablation depth of 30–40 μm with arithmetic sur­

compromise.

4.3.3. Model validation for polyethylene terephthalate (PET)
To further verify the universality of the model across different 
polymer substrates, polyethylene terephthalate (PET) was selected for 
subsequent validation. As a commonly used transparent polymer, PET 
exhibits distinct optical and thermal properties compared to PDMS, of­
fering a meaningful contrast for evaluating the model’s adaptability to 
varying material characteristics. In this section, a series of laser ablation 
experiments were conducted on PET, and the predicted ablation depths 
were systematically compared with experimental results to assess the 
robustness of the model.
Previous studies report that the absorption coefficient of PET at a 
wavelength of 1064 nm is approximately 0.133 cm−1 [52], which is 
significantly lower than that of PDMS. Consequently, PET absorbs less 
laser energy under identical conditions, making effective ablation more 
challenging. Its single-pulse ablation threshold under 1064 nm IR 
picosecond laser irradiation is reported to be 3.46 J/cm2 [53], which is 
higher than that of PDMS and consistent with its lower absorption 
capacity.
To accommodate the material-specific properties of PET, laser pa­
rameters were systematically varied. Laser power ranged from 3.68 W to 
4.95 W, with a constant scanning speed of 800 mm/s and a fixed line 
spacing of 7 μm. Line spacing was further adjusted from 5 μm to 10 μm in 
1 μm increments, with the laser power set at 4.54 W and the scanning 
speed held constant at 800 mm/s. In addition, the scanning speed was 
varied from 600 mm/s to 1200 mm/s in 200 mm/s increments, under a 
constant power of 4.54 W and a fixed line spacing of 7 μm. The corre­
sponding ablation depth results are presented in Fig. 12d–f, and the 
detailed experimental parameters are listed in Table A7.
Based on the analysis in Section 4.3.2 and considering the laser in­
cubation effect, an effective ablation fluence of 2.4 J/cm2 was adopted. 
This value corresponds to approximately 70 % of the single-pulse 
ablation threshold. A correction factor (ξ) of 0.28 was applied in the 
model. Under these conditions, the maximum relative prediction error 
was 4.37 %, and the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) was 
calculated to be 2.68 % (see Fig. 12d–f and Table A7). These results 
indicate that the predicted values closely match the experimental data, 
with minimal relative deviation, thereby confirming the reliability and 
accuracy of the theoretical model.
The experimental results indicate that the correction factors required 
for accurate ablation depth prediction vary between different polymer 
materials. For PDMS, the optimal correction factor was determined to be 
0.48, while for PET it was 0.28. These differences arise from the com­
bined influence of material properties and laser parameters. The 
correction factor is influenced by several factors, such as the material’s 
thermal conductivity, optical absorption, and the laser’s wavelength and 
pulse duration. Therefore, proper calibration is essential when applying 
the theoretical model across different polymer substrates to ensure 
predictive accuracy.
In summary, the established theoretical model demonstrates reliable 
and accurate predictive capability for laser ablation depth in both PDMS 
and PET polymer substrates. The predicted results show good agreement 
with experimental data across a wide range of fabricating parameters 
and material types. For PDMS, the mean absolute percentage error 
(MAPE) was 5.99 %, while for PET it was reduced to 2.68 %. Despite the 
distinct optical and thermal properties of PDMS and PET, the model 
remains robust and adaptable. Notably, the prediction errors observed 
in this study are closely with those reported for metal materials in pulsed 
laser ablation, where average errors typically remain below 10 % [54]. 
These experiments confirm the model’s applicability to different poly­
mer materials and suggest its strong potential for extension to a broader 
class of materials in laser micromachining applications.
4.4. Model-guided determination of dynamic shifting distance in laser 
fabrication
Based on the dynamic-focusing scanning fabrication strategy, we use 
the theoretical model to predict the ablation depth and determine the 
laser dynamic shifting parameters. After each layer is processed, the 
laser beam is refocused to maintain stable fabrication conditions. We 
fabricated tapered microstructures with a target height of 400 μm and 
width of 300 μm using nine different fabrication parameter sets. The 
predicted ablation depths from the theoretical model were used to 
determine the required number of fabrication layers, as detailed in 
Table A8. The 3D morphology of a representative tapered microstruc­
ture is shown in Fig. 13a. Cross-sectional profiles were measured at three 
distinct positions, and the corresponding 2D contour curves showed 
excellent consistency (Fig. 13b).
Furthermore, statistical analysis of the height and width of the mi­
crostructures fabricated under the nine parameter sets revealed high 
dimensional stability. The measured height consistently remained close 
to 400 μm (Fig. 13c), while the width showed minimal deviation from 
the nominal 300 μm, with an average of 298 μm (Fig. 13d). Additionally, 
we quantified the volume of individual tapered microstructures and 
found that the variations were within an acceptable range, with a rela­
tive error of less than 3 % (Fig. 13e). These findings demonstrate the 
reliability, consistency, and precision of the proposed laser fabrication 
method.
4.5. Fabrication accuracy and form deviation analysis: example of 3D 
pyramid structure design
To quantitatively evaluate the dimensional and form accuracy of 
laser-fabricated microstructures, a series of pyramid structures were 
designed as representative test geometries. Eight combinations were 
evaluated by varying two base widths (300 μm and 350 μm) with four 
structure heights (500, 400, 300, and 200 μm). Fabrication employed 
fixed laser parameters: 3.8 W power, 100 kHz repetition rate, 1000 mm/ 
s scanning speed, and 8 μm scanning spacing. We extracted the cross- 
sectional profiles of the fabricated pyramid microstructures (Fig. A4), 
and the measured profiles were compared with the corresponding 
design profiles to evaluate the fabrication accuracy.
Fig. 14 presents the measured and designed profiles across varying 
sizes. The left column (Fig. 14a–c, e, g) shows results for the 300 μm base 
width structures, while the right column (Fig. 14b–d, f, h) corresponds to

structure height (500 μm, 400 μm, 300 μm, and 200 μm, respectively). 
Six cross-sectional profiles were extracted from each group, and form 
accuracy was evaluated using peak-to-valley (PV) and root-mean-square

4.6. Fabrication of various types of 3D microstructures
4.6.1. Fabrication on polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) substrate
To demonstrate the flexibility and versatility of the 3D-DFL method, 
a variety of 3D microstructures were fabricated on PDMS substrates, as 
shown in Figs. 15 and 16. Fig. 15a–h illustrate tapered structures and 
frustum structures with triangular, quadrilateral, pentagonal, and 
conical geometries. According to the fabricating results, it was found 
that the laser shutdown delay affects the bottom ablation characteristics 
of microstructures. Appropriate shutdown delay can significantly reduce 
over ablation, as shown in Fig. A5. SEM images confirm the high geo­
metric fidelity and dimensional uniformity of these protruding features. 
These results demonstrate the method’s ability to precisely tailor 
microstructure dimensions through the combined control of structural 
design and laser fabricating parameters.
In addition to fabricating protruding structures, the 3D-DFL method 
was also employed to fabricate recessed microstructures, which present 
additional challenges such as ensuring stable depth modulation and 
avoiding over-ablation or geometric distortion. Arrays of recessed 
conical, triangular, quadrilateral, and pentagonal pyramid microstruc­
tures were successfully fabricated, as shown in Fig. 15i–l. The 3D mor­
phologies clearly reveal that the inner surfaces of the recessed structures 
are smooth and the geometries are sharply defined. These recessed ar­
chitectures are particularly promising for applications in micro-mold 
fabrication, where surface quality and dimensional accuracy are critical.
To expand the design versatility of the 3D-DFL approach, we fabri­
cated complex microstructures with functional configurations. Moir´e- 
patterned arrays (Fig. 16a and b), which show potential for optical 
modulation [32], were successfully generated with high geometric 
precision. Multi-scale tapered arrays and rhombic pyramid structures 
(Fig. 16c–f) were also fabricated, demonstrating potential applications 
in flexible electronics due to their scalable geometry and tunable surface 
topology. The methodology was further extended to fabricate 
MEMS-challenging 3D microstructures, including bidirectional alter­
nating wave structures, trident-shaped features, and other intricate 
forms (Fig. 16g–l). Such structures have potential applications in surface 
engineering, including microfluidics, directional wetting, and super­
hydrophobic surfaces.
Collectively, these results highlight the exceptional capability of the 
3D-DFL method in fabricating diverse structural typologies and pre­
cisely controlled feature dimensions. The approach enables high- 
fidelity, multi-scale, and application-oriented fabrication of advanced 
3D microsystems.

4.7.2. Microfluidic chips for rapid mixing of liquids
In microfluidics, liquid mixing is widely used to improve reaction 
efficiency, enhance measurement accuracy, and ensure uniformity of 
components. Fig. 18d shows a Y-shaped microfluidic mixer chip with 
serpentine channels, fabricated by laser fabricating on a PDMS substrate 
and plasma bonding to a glass substrate. The magnified views in Fig. 18e 
and f shows serpentine microchannels with and without disturbance 
plates. The smooth internal surfaces of the microchannels facilitate 
laminar flow. Red and blue liquids were pumped into the microchannel 
at a flow rate of 1 mL/h, and pixel analysis of the grayscale values at the 
outlet was performed to assess whether mixing occurred. In the micro­
channel without a disturbance plate, the liquid followed laminar flow, 
with no mixing observed. The grayscale value at the outlet decreased 
from 180 to 110, indicating clear layering and a lack of significant 
mixing (Fig. 18e and Video S1). However, the addition of a disturbance 
plate structure weakened the laminar flow, creating local flow 
obstruction and inducing flowline curvatures, vortices, or recirculation 
zones around the plate. The image at the outlet of the microchannel in 
Fig. 18f and Video S2 show clear evidence of mixing, with grayscale 
values between 140 and 120, confirming that the disturbance plate 
enhanced the mixing effect.
4.7.3. Ultrasonic droplet manipulation
Ultrasonic droplet manipulation technology has broad applications 
in biomedicine, drug delivery, and chemical synthesis. Fig. 18g shows 
the principle of ultrasonic droplet manipulation, wherein the key tech­
nology involves utilizing ultrasonic energy on a laser-fabricated super­
hydrophobic surface to generate an upward acoustic radiation force, 
enabling the levitation of a droplet from the superhydrophobic surface. 
Fig. 18h shows the experimental process of droplet manipulation. The 
ultrasonic transducer is moved to the left, positioned above the red- 
colored droplet. Upon activation of the ultrasound, the 30 μL droplet 
becomes suspended. The transducer then moves 30 mm to the right, 
with the red-colored droplet following the transducer. When the droplet 
comes into contact with the blue-colored droplet, the two droplets mix, 
turning brown, while maintaining suspension, as depicted in Video S3.
5. Conclusion
This study addresses the challenges in fabricating 3D functional 
microstructures on transparent polymers, aiming to enhance machining 
precision and tailoring capabilities. By employing a 3D dynamic- 
focusing laser (3D-DFL) approach with an IR picosecond laser, we ach­
ieved high-precision microfabrication of transparent polymer. The ma­
terial removal mechanisms under IR femtosecond laser ablation were 
revealed. A comprehensive analysis of the impact of focus increment 
was conducted, investigating both the macroscopic structural evolution 
during the fabrication process and the microstructural characteristics of 
the resulting surfaces. A theoretical model for material removal during 
laser-material interactions was presented. For the first time, 3D micro­
structures with high geometric accuracy and consistency were success­
fully fabricated on a transparent polymer, using polydimethylsiloxane 
(PDMS) and polyethylene terephthalate (PET) as a case study. The main 
conclusions are as follows. 
1. A customized IR picosecond 3D dynamic-focusing laser process was 
presented for fabricating transparent flexible polymer materials. The 
laser fabrication process was observed online using a high-speed 
camera, revealing the material’s three-stage ablation process of 
“stabilization”, “expansion”, and “contraction” under laser ablation, 
offering new insights into thermomechanical responses in trans­
parent soft materials under ultrafast laser irradiation.
2. The mismatch between focus increments and actual ablation depths 
was identified as a critical factor affecting ablation quality. Positive 
defocusing promotes the formation of recast layers and an increase in

3. A theoretical model for predicting ablation depth was established to 
achieve accurate matching between dynamic focus and the actual

and applications in ultrasonic droplet manipulation stage for non- 
contact liquid control. These examples demonstrate the method’s 
scalability and adaptability, highlighting its potential to impact 
diverse fields such as flexible electronics, optics, microfluidics, and 
biomaterials engineering.
These findings demonstrate that 3D dynamic focal control provides a 
versatile strategy for addressing key challenges in laser fabrication of 
transparent materials. The proposed laser-based approach not only en­

polymers, but also shows potential for broader applications involving 
semitransparent ceramics, biomaterials, and other hard-to-machine 
substrates. Further experimental studies are needed to determine

Appendix 10. The structure design and the working principle of the sensor
The sensor structure is illustrated in Fig. A6. It comprises interdigital electrodes, a conductive sensing layer with a pyramid microstructure, and 
protective packaging layers. The sensing layer is sprayed with carbon black (CB)/polyethylene oxide (PEO) conductive material. A polyimide (PI) film 
is applied to form a “sandwich” packaging configuration, which securely bonds the sensing layer to the interdigital electrodes. Furthermore, the PI film 
protects the sensor from humidity and environmental fluctuations, thereby enhancing its stability.
Sensitivity is a key parameter of sensor performance. The sensitivity is calculated as follow: 
S = d(ΔI/I0)
d(Δp)
(S1) 
Where S is the sensitivity of the pressure sensor, defined as the slope of the electrical response curve, ΔI is the amount of change in the output signal of 
the pressure sensor, I0 is the initial value of the current of the pressure sensor in the absence of external pressure, which is generally determined by the 
preparation process and the material, and Δp is the amount of change in the external pressure at the time of the change in the output of ΔI from the 
pressure sensor. From Eq. (S1), to improve the sensitivity, the change in the resistance of the pressure sensor under a certain pressure input needs to be 
increased.
The total resistance (Rt) of the sensor consists of the interdigital electrode resistance (Rm), the microstructure sensing layer resistance (Rh) and the 
contact resistance (Rc) constituting the total, as shown in equation (S2): 
Rt = Rm + Rh + Rc
(S2) 
The contact resistance (Rc) can be expressed as: 
Rc =
1
1
Rc1 +
1
Rc2 + … + 1
Rcx
(S3) 
The total contact resistance (Rc) between the interdigital electrodes and the pyramid-shaped sensing layer is calculated as the sum of the contact 
resistances at each contact point (Rcx), where x denotes the total number of contact points. The corresponding equivalent circuit model is illustrated in 
Fig. A7a. The sensing mechanism of the pyramid microstructure is shown in Fig. A7b. Upon the application of external pressure, the pyramid 
structures gradually deform, leading to an increase in the area between the sensing layer and the electrode surface. This change effectively reduces the 
overall contact resistance and enhances the sensor’s pressure sensitivity. Additionally, the interdigital electrodes are made of highly conductive metal, 
resulting in negligible internal resistance (Rm). Consequently, the total resistance (Rt) decreases with increasing applied pressure, following the 
deformation of the pyramid microstructures.
